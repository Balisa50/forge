===D1===
## When the answer depends on who is asking
Every model so far gave the same answer for the same input: a flight's features map to one delay probability, regardless of who asks. **Recommender systems** break that, the prediction depends not just on the item but on the *specific user*, so "should we show this movie?" has a different answer for every person. They are how Netflix decides what to show you, Amazon what to suggest, Spotify what goes in your Discover Weekly, one of the most commercially valuable ML applications and one of the most technically interesting, because personalisation creates a high-dimensional problem that standard classification and regression cannot easily solve. This week you build a recommender and learn the ideas, collaborative filtering, embeddings, cold-start, exploration, diversity, that make personalised prediction work.

## Why recommendation is a different kind of problem
A recommender is not just a classifier with the user as an extra feature, the structure of the problem is fundamentally different, and naming why frames the week. The core object is the **user-item interaction matrix**: rows are users, columns are items, and entries are interactions (a watch, a rating, a click). Recommendation is the problem of *filling in the blanks*, predicting which of the vast number of items a user has *not* interacted with they would like. This is different from classification because there is no fixed set of labels, every user-item pair is a potential prediction, and the "right answer" is personal. It is high-dimensional (millions of users times hundreds of thousands of items) and the goal, surfacing the few items each user will love from an enormous catalogue, does not fit the predict-one-target shape of the models you have built. So recommenders need their own methods, which is what makes them a distinct and rich topic.

## The defining challenge: sparsity
The single fact that shapes all of recommendation is the **sparsity** of the interaction matrix, and the numbers are staggering. A platform with 1 million users and 100,000 titles has 100 *billion* possible user-item interactions, but the actual data (the watches, ratings, clicks that really happened) might be 0.1% of that, around 100 million observations in a 100-billion-entry matrix. So 99.9% of the matrix is *empty*, and recommendation is the task of intelligently filling that vast emptiness, predicting preferences for the overwhelming majority of pairs you have *no* direct data on, by generalising from the tiny fraction you do. Any method that needed explicit data for each user-item pair would be hopeless; the whole art is generalising from 0.1% to the other 99.9%. This sparsity is why recommendation needs clever techniques (collaborative filtering, embeddings) that find structure in the sparse data and extrapolate from it, and it is the constraint every idea this week is designed to overcome.

===D2===
## Collaborative filtering: people like you liked this
The foundational recommendation technique is **collaborative filtering**, and its idea is intuitive: recommend to a user based on the preferences of *similar* users (or similar items). "People who liked what you liked also liked X, so you might like X too." It comes in two complementary forms:
- **User-based:** find users similar to you (they watched/rated the same things you did), and recommend items *they* liked that you have not seen. Your taste-neighbours' favourites become your recommendations.
- **Item-based:** find items similar to ones you liked (items that tend to be liked by the same people), and recommend those. "Because you watched this, here is a similar one."
The remarkable thing is what collaborative filtering does *not* need: it requires *no* information about the items themselves (no genre, no description) or the users (no demographics), only the interaction patterns, who interacted with what. It finds taste structure purely from the matrix of interactions, which is why it is the workhorse of recommendation.

## Why "collaborative" and why it works on sparse data
The name "collaborative" captures the mechanism: users implicitly *collaborate* by their collective behaviour, your recommendations come from the patterns in everyone's interactions pooled together. This is exactly how it overcomes the sparsity problem from yesterday: even though *you* have interacted with only a tiny fraction of items, *many other users collectively* have interacted with most items, so by finding users similar to you, the system borrows their experience to predict your preferences for items you have never touched. You have not watched item X, but a hundred users with taste like yours have, and they loved it, so it recommends X to you. The structure in the sparse matrix, the fact that users cluster into taste groups and items cluster into preference groups, is what makes generalising from 0.1% possible: you do not need *your* data on an item, you need data from *people like you* on that item, which collectively exists. Collaborative filtering exploits exactly this, finding and using the latent taste structure that pervades the interaction data, which is why it works despite the matrix being almost entirely empty.

## The strengths and limits of collaborative filtering
Collaborative filtering's great strength is that it needs only interaction data and discovers preferences you could never specify by hand, it can surface that fans of one obscure thing tend to love another seemingly-unrelated thing, purely from behaviour, with no notion of *why*. This serendipity (recommending things that are not obviously similar by any content feature, but that similar people love) is a major advantage over content-based approaches and a big part of why it powers real recommenders. Its limits motivate the rest of the week. It struggles with **sparsity at the extremes** (a brand-new user or item with no interactions has no neighbours, the cold-start problem of D4), it can be **computationally heavy** at scale (finding similar users among millions is expensive, which is exactly what the embedding approach in D3 makes efficient), and it tends to recommend **popular** items (which many people interacted with), risking a lack of diversity (D6). So collaborative filtering is the powerful foundation, recommend based on collective taste structure in the interaction matrix, and the subsequent lessons address its weaknesses: embeddings make it scalable and richer, cold-start handling covers the no-data cases, and diversity/exploration counter its popularity bias. Understanding collaborative filtering, and *why* it works on sparse data, is the conceptual core of recommendation.

## Where this fits

===D3===
## Modern recommenders: embeddings
Classic collaborative filtering compares users and items directly, which is expensive at scale. The modern approach represents users and items as **embeddings**, dense vectors in a shared "taste space", learned so that a user's vector and an item's vector are *close* when the user likes the item. You met embeddings in the deep-learning phase (and in the AI-engineering RAG work); here they power recommendation:
```text
each user -> a vector (their position in "taste space")
each item -> a vector (its position in the same space)
predicted preference(user, item) = similarity(user_vector, item_vector)   (e.g. dot product)
recommend the items whose vectors are closest to the user's vector
```
The model *learns* these vectors from the interaction data, placing users and items so that observed likes correspond to nearby vectors, and then a user's recommendations are simply the items nearest their vector.

## Why embeddings are the right representation
Embeddings solve collaborative filtering's problems elegantly, which is why they dominate modern recommenders:
- **They handle sparsity by learning latent structure.** The model compresses each user and item into a low-dimensional vector that captures the *essential* taste factors (this user likes action and sci-fi; this item is action sci-fi), generalising from the sparse interactions to fill the empty matrix, this is the same latent-factor idea as matrix factorisation, the classic embedding-based recommender. A user's vector summarises their taste from their few interactions, and it then predicts their preference for *any* item via vector similarity.
- **They are efficient at scale.** Once users and items are vectors, recommendation becomes a *nearest-neighbour search* in vector space, find the items closest to the user's vector, which is exactly the fast approximate-nearest-neighbour problem you saw in RAG (the same vector-search infrastructure, HNSW and friends). So instead of comparing a user against millions of others directly, you do an efficient vector lookup, making real-time recommendation over huge catalogues feasible.
- **They blend signals naturally.** Embeddings can incorporate more than interactions, item content, user features, context, into the learned vectors (hybrid recommenders), and they slot into neural recommendation models, the current state of the art.

## The unifying idea: preference as geometry
The deep idea embeddings bring to recommendation is **turning preference into geometry**: by placing users and items in a shared vector space where proximity means affinity, you convert the messy, sparse, high-dimensional recommendation problem into a clean geometric one, recommendation is "find the items near this user." This is the same powerful move you saw with embeddings for text in RAG (meaning becomes proximity) and it is why embeddings are one of the most important ideas in modern ML: they represent complex objects (words, images, users, items) as vectors whose *distances encode relationships*, turning hard problems into tractable nearest-neighbour searches. For recommendation specifically, this geometry is what makes a system both *accurate* (the learned space captures real taste structure from sparse data) and *fast* (recommendation is vector search), and it unifies recommendation with the broader embedding-based ML you have been learning. The takeaway is that a modern recommender learns a taste-space embedding for every user and item and recommends by proximity, which scales to millions of users and items in real time and generalises gracefully across the sparse matrix, the embedding being the representation that makes personalisation tractable.

## Where this fits
You understand embedding-based recommendation: learning user and item vectors in a shared taste space where proximity means preference, which handles sparsity (latent factors), scales via fast nearest-neighbour search (the RAG vector infrastructure), and blends signals, turning recommendation into geometry. This is the modern recommender's core. ===D4===
## The no-data problem: cold-start
Collaborative filtering and embeddings both learn from interactions, so they fail for the entities that *have no interactions yet*: a **brand-new user** who has done nothing, and a **brand-new item** nobody has touched. This is the **cold-start problem**, and it is a real, constant challenge because new users and items arrive continuously, a streaming service signs up users every minute and adds titles every week, and you must recommend *something* good to them despite having zero collaborative data. With no interactions, there are no taste-neighbours to borrow from and no learned embedding, so the methods that power your recommender simply have nothing to work with for these entities.

## How to recommend with no interaction data
Cold-start is solved by falling back on information *other than* interactions, and the techniques map to the two cold cases:
- **New user cold-start:** since you have no behaviour, use whatever you *do* know, any signup information (stated preferences, demographics), the context (device, location, time), or simply recommend **popular** items (the broadly-liked safe bets) until the user interacts enough to personalise. You can also *actively* learn fast, ask a new user to pick a few favourites or rate a handful of items during onboarding (the "choose 3 shows you like" flow), bootstrapping a taste signal immediately.
- **New item cold-start:** since nobody has interacted with it, use the item's **content** (genre, description, attributes, creator) to place it near similar items that *do* have interactions, this is **content-based** recommendation, and it is exactly why hybrid systems matter. A new sci-fi film with no watches can still be recommended to sci-fi fans because its *content* matches items they liked.
The common thread is **substitute information for missing interactions**: content features for new items, profile/context/popularity for new users, and active solicitation to gather signal fast.

## Why hybrid systems are the real answer
The deep lesson of cold-start is that **no single recommendation approach is sufficient, and real systems are hybrids** that combine collaborative filtering (powerful when interaction data exists) with content-based methods (which work from the start, using item/user attributes). This is the recommendation version of the recurring "match the method to the situation" theme: collaborative filtering shines once there is interaction data but cannot start cold, while content-based recommendation works immediately from attributes but misses the serendipitous, behaviour-driven discoveries collaborative filtering excels at, so combining them gives you both, content-based to cover cold-start and collaborative to deliver rich personalisation as data accumulates. A new user gets popularity-and-onboarding-based recommendations that quickly become collaborative as they interact; a new item gets content-based exposure that becomes collaborative once it gathers interactions. This graceful transition from content-based (cold) to collaborative (warm) as data accumulates is how production recommenders handle the constant arrival of new users and items, and it is why "which technique?" is the wrong question, the answer is a hybrid that uses each where it is strong. Understanding cold-start, and that hybrids solve it, is understanding why real recommenders are never a single algorithm but a combination engineered to handle the full lifecycle from no-data to rich-data.

## Where this fits
You understand the cold-start problem, no interaction data for new users and items, and its solutions: substitute other information (content features for new items, profile/context/popularity and active onboarding for new users), unified in *hybrid* systems that combine content-based (works cold) with collaborative (rich when warm). ===D5===
## The recommender's dilemma: exploit or explore
A recommender that only ever recommends what it is *most confident* you will like has a hidden flaw: it never learns about your other tastes, and it traps you in a narrow loop. This is the **exploration vs exploitation** trade-off, one of the most important dynamics in recommendation:
- **Exploitation:** recommend the items the model is confident the user will like (based on what it has learned), maximising immediate satisfaction.
- **Exploration:** sometimes recommend items the model is *uncertain* about, to *learn* more about the user's preferences (and to gather data on lesser-known items), at the cost of some immediate hit-rate.
A recommender must do both: pure exploitation gives reliable but stale, narrowing recommendations; pure exploration gives random, unsatisfying ones; the art is balancing them.

## Why exploration is necessary, not wasteful
It is tempting to think exploration is just serving worse recommendations, but it is essential for the system's long-term quality, and understanding why is the insight. Three reasons exploitation alone fails: First, the model only *knows* what it has seen the user interact with, so without exploration it can never discover that you would *love* a genre you have never been shown, it keeps recommending more of the same and never learns your broader taste, a self-reinforcing filter bubble. Second, **new items need exposure to gather data** (the cold-start connection): if the recommender only exploits known-popular items, new items never get shown, never accumulate interactions, and never get a chance, so exploration is how the catalogue beyond the popular head gets discovered. Third, **users and tastes change**, and a purely exploitative model, anchored to past behaviour, never notices, where exploration keeps probing and adapting. So exploration is an *investment*: you give up a little immediate satisfaction now to learn information that makes *future* recommendations better, the same way an organisation invests in R&D. A recommender that never explores slowly degrades into a narrow, stale echo of the user's past, even as it looks "accurate" by exploiting known preferences, which connects to the offline-online gap from W19 (a model that exploits past clicks scores well offline but may engage users less than one that explores).

## Balancing the trade: bandits and beyond
The exploration-exploitation trade-off is formalised by **multi-armed bandit** algorithms, which provide principled ways to balance trying uncertain options against exploiting known-good ones, and you do not need the full theory to grasp the approaches: **epsilon-greedy** (mostly exploit, but a small fraction of the time pick something to explore), **upper-confidence-bound** (favour items that are either known-good *or* highly uncertain, optimistic about the unknown), and **Thompson sampling** (sample from your uncertainty about each option's value, naturally exploring uncertain ones), the Bayesian flavour connecting to W19's Bayesian thinking. The practical takeaway is that a good recommender *deliberately* injects exploration, a controlled fraction of "let's try something to learn", rather than purely exploiting, and tunes how much based on the cost of a worse recommendation versus the value of learning. This is the same explore-to-learn-vs-exploit-to-earn trade that appears across decision-making under uncertainty, and recognising it in recommendation explains a lot of real-system behaviour (why Spotify sometimes serves you something unexpected, it is exploring). The deeper point is that a recommender is not a static predictor but an *interactive learner* that shapes the very data it learns from (it only sees interactions on what it recommends), so it must explore to avoid starving itself of the information it needs to improve, a feedback loop no static model has. Balancing exploration and exploitation is what keeps a recommender learning, fresh, and able to surface the catalogue beyond the obvious.

## Beyond accuracy: diversity and serendipity
A recommender optimised purely for *accuracy* (predict what the user will click) can produce a subtly bad experience: a list of items that are all *correct* but all *the same*, ten action movies, or ten variations of what you already watched, technically accurate, practically boring and unhelpful. Two qualities beyond accuracy fix this:
- **Diversity:** the recommendations should *vary*, span different genres, styles, and types, rather than being ten near-identical items. A diverse list is more useful and more interesting, and more likely to contain something the user is in the mood for right now.
- **Serendipity:** recommendations should sometimes be *pleasantly surprising*, items the user would not have found themselves and would not expect, but ends up loving. Serendipity is the delight of discovery, and it is much of what makes a recommender feel magical rather than obvious.
Optimising accuracy alone delivers neither, which is why good recommenders explicitly value diversity and serendipity, not just hit-rate.

## Why accuracy alone is the wrong objective
The key insight is that **the metric you optimise (accuracy) is not the same as the experience you want (a satisfying, engaging recommendation)**, exactly the metric-outcome gap from the A/B-testing week, here within recommendation. A recommender that maximises predicted-click accuracy tends to recommend the *obvious* and the *similar*: items so close to what the user already engaged with that the prediction is safe, which is accurate but redundant (you would have found those anyway, so the recommender added little value) and narrowing (it keeps showing more of the same, the filter-bubble effect). Real value often comes from the *non-obvious* good recommendation, the diverse option that matches your current mood, the serendipitous discovery you would never have found, and these score *lower* on naive accuracy (they are less predictable) while delivering *higher* real satisfaction and engagement. This is why, just as in W19, the offline-accurate model can be the online-worse model: the most "accurate" recommendations (predicting your obvious next click) may engage you *less* than a diverse, serendipitous set. So diversity and serendipity are not soft niceties; they are corrections to accuracy's blind spot, ensuring the recommender delivers *useful* and *delightful* recommendations rather than merely *predictable* ones.

## Balancing the objectives, and the human stakes
In practice you *balance* accuracy with diversity and serendipity, you do not abandon accuracy (irrelevant recommendations are bad too), but you deliberately trade a little predicted-accuracy for variety and surprise, tuning the mix to maximise *real* engagement and satisfaction (measured, as W19 taught, by online outcomes, not offline accuracy). Techniques include re-ranking a candidate list to spread genres, penalising too-similar items, and injecting exploratory or serendipitous picks (connecting to D5's exploration, exploration *produces* serendipity). There is also a genuine **human dimension** worth naming: recommenders that optimise narrow accuracy can create *filter bubbles* (showing people only more of what they already engage with, narrowing their world) and amplify popularity (the rich get richer, niche content starves), which has real effects on what people discover, what creators get seen, and even on society (news and information recommenders shaping what people are exposed to). So building in diversity is not only about engagement metrics, it is also a responsibility, countering the narrowing and homogenising tendencies that pure accuracy optimisation produces. This connects forward to the Responsible ML phase (fairness, the societal impact of ML systems): a recommender is one of the most influential ML systems in people's daily lives, and designing it to be diverse and serendipitous, not just accurate, is both better product and more responsible engineering. The mature recommender optimises for satisfying discovery, balancing accuracy, diversity, and serendipity, not for the hollow accuracy of predicting the obvious.
