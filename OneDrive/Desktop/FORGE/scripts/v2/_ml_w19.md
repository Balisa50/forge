===D1===
## The gap between offline metrics and real value
You have trusted held-out test metrics all track, and they are essential, but this week confronts their limit: **you cannot trust offline metrics to predict online performance.** A model that scores 0.79 AUC on a held-out test set *may or may not* be better, for real users making real decisions, than the model currently in production. The gap is real and it has causes: your test set was sampled from the *same* distribution as your training data (so it may not reflect live conditions), real users behave differently than the historical data suggests, and distribution shift between when you trained and when you serve means offline performance does not cleanly transfer to online outcomes. **A/B testing** is the method that generates statistically valid evidence about which model produces better outcomes *in the real world*, with actual users, which is the only verdict that ultimately matters.

## Why a better offline score is not a better model
It is worth sitting with *why* a higher test AUC does not guarantee a better production model, because it is counterintuitive after weeks of optimising offline metrics. First, the **test set is not the world**: it was drawn from your training distribution, so a model that scores higher on it may simply fit that historical distribution better, not the live one users actually generate. Second, **the metric is not the outcome**: AUC measures ranking quality, but the *business* cares about real outcomes, revenue, user satisfaction, decisions made, and a model with higher AUC might not move those, or might even hurt them (a "better" recommender by offline accuracy might show less diverse items and reduce engagement). Third, **users react to the model**: in production the model influences behaviour (people respond to its predictions/recommendations), a feedback loop that no static offline test can capture. So offline metrics are a necessary *filter* (a model that fails offline is not worth deploying) but not a *sufficient* verdict (passing offline does not prove it helps in production). The only way to know if a model genuinely produces better real-world outcomes is to test it on real users, which is A/B testing.

## What A/B testing does
A/B testing answers the real question, "does model B produce better outcomes than model A for real users?", by running a controlled experiment in production: you split live traffic, send some users to the current model (A, the control) and some to the new model (B, the treatment), and compare the *real outcomes* they produce, with **statistical rigour** to know whether any difference is genuine or just noise. The output is statistically valid evidence, "model B increased the outcome we care about by X%, and we are confident this is real, not chance", which is exactly the basis on which you should decide to roll out a new model. This week you learn to do it properly: why offline is not enough (the foundation), the statistical tests that make the comparison valid, Bayesian alternatives, experimentation platforms, and holdouts. The throughline is that **deciding to deploy a new model is an experiment, and it deserves the rigour of one**, not a leap of faith from an offline score.

===D2===
## Why offline metrics are necessary but not sufficient
Today examines the central premise carefully, because getting it right shapes how you ship every model. Offline metrics (test-set AUC, accuracy) are **necessary**: a model that performs poorly on held-out data is not worth deploying, so offline evaluation is the essential first filter, do not A/B test a model that fails offline. But they are **not sufficient** to conclude a model is better in production, and naming the specific reasons makes the gap concrete:
- **Distribution shift:** your test set came from the training distribution, but production data can differ (the drift from W18), so offline performance may not hold live.
- **Metric-outcome mismatch:** offline metrics measure statistical quality (AUC, accuracy), but the business cares about *outcomes* (revenue, retention, decisions), and these do not move in lockstep, a model can win on AUC and lose on the outcome that matters.
- **User feedback effects:** in production, users *react* to the model's outputs, creating dynamics no static test captures.
So offline metrics tell you a model is *plausible*, not that it is *better* for real users.

## The offline-online gap is the heart of it
The single most important idea this week is the **offline-online gap**: the difference between how a model performs on your test set and how it performs in production, and the recognition that this gap can be large, even inverting which model is "better." A model with higher test AUC can produce *worse* business outcomes, and a model with slightly lower offline metrics can perform *better* live, because offline and online measure different things under different conditions. Concrete example: two recommenders, model B has higher offline accuracy (it predicts what users already clicked), but model A, with slightly lower offline accuracy, recommends more diverse items that users actually engage with more, so A wins online despite losing offline. The offline metric rewarded predicting the past; the online outcome rewarded driving future engagement, and they disagreed. Internalising that this gap exists, and can be large enough to reverse your decision, is what motivates A/B testing: you cannot close the gap by thinking harder about offline metrics; you can only measure across it by testing in production. The teams that ship confidently are the ones who treat the offline score as a hypothesis and the A/B test as the verdict.

## What this means for how you ship models
The practical consequence is a two-stage shipping discipline that respects both kinds of evidence. **Offline first, as a filter:** use held-out metrics (and your W13 experiment tracking) to develop, compare, and select candidate models, only the ones that clearly pass offline are worth the cost of a production test. **Online second, as the verdict:** A/B test the offline-winning candidate against the current production model on real users, and let the *online* outcome decide whether to roll it out. This respects that offline evaluation is cheap and fast (so you do most of your iteration there) while online evaluation is the true measure (so the final decision rests on it). It also means you must define, *before* the test, what *online* outcome you are optimising, the real metric (engagement, revenue, decision quality) that the model is meant to improve, not just AUC, so the A/B test measures what actually matters. This is the same "define success measurably before you start" discipline from the AI-engineering capstone, applied to model rollout. The mature ML engineer never deploys a new model on offline metrics alone, nor wastes A/B tests on models that fail offline, but uses offline to filter and online to decide.

===D3===
## Making the comparison valid: two-sample tests
An A/B test splits users between model A and model B and compares their outcomes, but you cannot just look at "B's average was higher" and declare B the winner, because of **noise**: random variation means B's group could look better by chance even if the models are identical. **Two-sample statistical tests** tell you whether an observed difference between the two groups is *real* (statistically significant) or could easily be random. The test answers: "given the variation in the data, how likely is a difference this big if the two models were actually the same?", and only if that is unlikely do you conclude B is genuinely better.
```text
A (control):   outcomes from users on the current model
B (treatment): outcomes from users on the new model
two-sample test -> is B's difference REAL, or could it be chance?
```

## ML A/B tests are harder than web A/B tests
A crucial point that makes this week non-trivial: model A/B testing is statistically *harder* than the classic web A/B test, and knowing why tells you which test to use. Web A/B tests usually measure a **binary** outcome (clicked or not), with well-known statistics and modest sample-size needs. Model A/B tests often measure **continuous** outcomes (a quality score, revenue per prediction) or **rare events** (default rate, fraud rate), and these change everything:
- **Continuous outcomes** need different tests (comparing *means*, e.g. a t-test, with attention to the variance) than binary ones, and the high variance of quality scores means you need more data to detect a difference.
- **Rare events** (a 1% default rate) need *large* samples to measure reliably, you need many observations to see enough of the rare event to compare rates with confidence, so the test can take a long time to reach a verdict.
So you must match the statistical test to the *kind* of outcome you are measuring (binary, continuous, or rare-event), and recognise that ML outcomes often demand larger samples and longer tests than web experiments, which is a practical constraint on how fast you can validate a model.

## Sample size, significance, and the discipline of honest testing
Two-sample testing comes with disciplines that protect you from fooling yourself, the same rigour as any experiment. **Sample size:** you need enough observations for the test to reliably detect a difference of the size you care about, too few and a real improvement is lost in noise (and a result is meaningless), so you estimate the required sample size *before* running (based on the effect size you want to detect and the outcome's variability) and run until you reach it. **Statistical significance:** you set a threshold (commonly p < 0.05) for how unlikely a chance result must be before you believe the difference is real, and only conclude B wins if the test clears it. And critically, **do not peek and stop early:** repeatedly checking the test and stopping the moment it looks significant dramatically inflates false positives (you will eventually see noise that looks like a win), so you decide the sample size and duration up front and wait for it, rather than calling the test the first time B looks ahead. These disciplines, adequate sample size, a pre-set significance threshold, and no early peeking, are what make an A/B test *valid* rather than a way to rationalise a decision you already wanted, and they are exactly where naive A/B testing goes wrong. The statistical rigour is not pedantry; it is what makes the test's verdict trustworthy.

===D4===
## A different lens: Bayesian A/B testing
The two-sample (frequentist) approach answers "is this difference statistically significant?" with p-values and fixed sample sizes. **Bayesian A/B testing** reframes the question into one that is often more directly useful: instead of "is the difference significant?", it answers **"what is the probability that B is better than A, and by how much?"** It does this by maintaining probability distributions over each model's true performance and updating them as data arrives, yielding statements like "there is a 95% probability that B is better than A, and the most likely improvement is 3%." For a decision-maker, that is a more natural and actionable answer than a p-value, it speaks directly to the choice you actually face (should I roll out B?).

## Why the Bayesian framing helps in practice
Bayesian A/B testing has practical advantages that matter for model rollout, which is why many modern experimentation platforms use it:
- **Directly interpretable results:** "95% probability B is better, expected lift 3%" is something a stakeholder immediately understands and can act on, where "p = 0.04" requires statistical translation and is famously easy to misinterpret.
- **More graceful with continuous monitoring:** the rigid frequentist "do not peek" rule (D3) exists because repeated checking inflates false positives; Bayesian methods handle ongoing monitoring more naturally, you can update your belief as data flows in and reason about the current probability, which fits the reality of watching an experiment, though you still need discipline not to stop on noise.
- **Incorporates magnitude and uncertainty:** it tells you not just *whether* B is better but *by how much* and *how sure* you are, which supports better decisions (a 0.5% improvement that is "probably real" may not be worth a rollout's risk, while a likely 5% gain is).
- **Handles small samples and priors:** it can incorporate prior knowledge and gives sensible answers even with limited data, degrading gracefully rather than just saying "not significant."

## Frequentist vs Bayesian: not a war, a choice of framing
The honest takeaway is that frequentist (two-sample tests) and Bayesian A/B testing are two valid frameworks answering slightly different questions, and you should understand both rather than dogmatically pick a side. Frequentist testing is the long-standing standard, well-understood, and what many organisations and tools use; its discipline (fixed sample size, significance threshold) is rigorous and widely accepted. Bayesian testing offers more directly interpretable, decision-friendly outputs and handles monitoring and magnitude more naturally, which is why it has grown popular in modern experimentation platforms. In practice you often will not implement either from scratch, the platforms (tomorrow) provide them, but understanding *what each tells you* lets you read and trust their results: a frequentist result says "the difference is unlikely to be chance," a Bayesian result says "here is the probability B is better and by how much." For making a *rollout decision*, the Bayesian framing is frequently more useful because it directly quantifies the decision-relevant question, but the rigour and acceptance of frequentist testing keep it the default in many settings. Knowing both, and that they are complementary lenses on the same underlying question (is B really better?), is the statistical literacy that lets you run and interpret model A/B tests competently. The point is never the framework for its own sake; it is making a *valid, well-founded decision* about whether to roll out a model.

===D5===
## Experimentation platforms: A/B testing as infrastructure
Running A/B tests properly, splitting traffic correctly, assigning users consistently, collecting outcomes, computing valid statistics, managing many concurrent experiments, is a substantial system, and **experimentation platforms** (in-house ones at big tech companies, and tools/services like Optimizely, GrowthBook, Statsig, LaunchDarkly's experimentation, and others) provide it as infrastructure. A platform handles the machinery: consistent **traffic assignment** (each user reliably gets the same variant), **experiment management** (define, launch, monitor, and stop experiments), correct **statistical analysis** (frequentist or Bayesian, computed properly), and **guardrails** (watching that a variant is not harming key metrics). You define the experiment and the metrics; the platform runs it rigorously.

## What the platform gets right that ad-hoc testing gets wrong
The reason to use a platform rather than hand-rolling A/B tests is that there are many subtle ways to run an experiment *wrong*, and the platform encodes the correct handling of each, exactly the value of dedicated infrastructure seen throughout the track. Consistent assignment: a user must get the *same* variant on every visit (flipping them between A and B mid-experiment corrupts the data), which requires careful, sticky, hashed assignment the platform manages. Correct randomisation: traffic must be split without bias (no systematic difference between groups except the model), which is easy to botch by hand. Proper statistics: the platform applies the right test for the outcome type and guards against the early-peeking and multiple-comparison traps from D3 (running many experiments inflates false positives unless corrected). Guardrail metrics: the platform watches that the new variant is not quietly tanking a critical business metric even if it improves the target one, automatically stopping a harmful experiment. Hand-rolling all of this correctly is hard and error-prone, and a botched A/B test gives a *confidently wrong* answer (worse than no test, because you trust it), so the platform's rigor is exactly what makes the experiment's verdict trustworthy.

## Using platforms well, and the gradual-rollout pattern
The practical guidance is to use an experimentation platform for any serious model A/B testing (do not hand-roll the statistics and assignment for a decision that matters), while matching the tool to your scale, a simple open-source experimentation tool for a small setup, a full platform for an organisation running many experiments. A pattern these platforms enable, and that is the safe way to actually roll out a model, is the **gradual rollout** combined with A/B testing: rather than flipping all traffic to the new model at once, you ramp it up, send 1% of traffic to model B, watch the metrics and guardrails, then 5%, 20%, 50%, and finally 100%, stopping or rolling back instantly if anything degrades. This limits the blast radius of a bad model (only a small fraction of users are affected if B is worse), gathers A/B evidence as you go, and is the standard safe-deployment practice for ML in production (the same canary/gradual-rollout idea from deployment engineering, here driven by experiment results). So the platform does not just measure the A/B test; it operationalises a safe, evidence-driven rollout. The takeaway is that A/B testing and rollout are infrastructure-supported processes, and using that infrastructure (platforms, gradual rollouts, guardrails) is how you ship new models safely and on real evidence, rather than via a risky all-at-once switch justified by an offline score.

## Measuring long-term, cumulative value: holdouts
A/B tests measure a single experiment over a defined window, but some effects only show up over the *long term* and *across many model changes*, and **holdouts** are the technique for measuring those. A holdout is a small group of users **permanently** (or for a long period) kept on a baseline, e.g. the old model, or no personalisation at all, while everyone else gets your continuously-improving models, so by comparing the holdout group against everyone else you measure the *cumulative* value of all your model work over time, not just one experiment's incremental gain.
```text
99% of users:  receive your evolving, improving models (many A/B-tested changes)
1% holdout:    kept on the baseline, long-term
compare them  -> the TOTAL, cumulative value of all your ML work
```

## Why individual A/B tests miss the long-term picture
Holdouts exist because a series of individual A/B tests can each show a small win while *missing or overstating* the true long-term, aggregate impact, and understanding this gap is the insight. Three problems holdouts address: First, **small wins compound or wash out** in ways single tests do not capture, ten experiments each showing +1% might sum to +10%, or might overlap and deliver far less, and only a long-term holdout measures the *actual* cumulative effect of all of them together. Second, **novelty and long-term effects differ from short-term ones**: a model change might boost a metric briefly (users react to novelty) but fade, or conversely build value slowly, and a short A/B test sees only the immediate effect, while a holdout reveals the sustained one. Third, **drift in the baseline**: without a held-out baseline, you lose track of how far you have actually come, the holdout is a stable reference point showing the total distance between "all our ML" and "the baseline." So holdouts answer a question individual A/B tests cannot: *across everything we have shipped, over the long run, how much value has our ML work actually created?*, which is the question leadership and you ultimately care about.

## The cost of holdouts, and using them wisely
Holdouts have a real, honest cost that you weigh deliberately: the holdout users receive a *worse* (baseline) experience, so you are deliberately giving a fraction of users an inferior product to measure your cumulative impact, which has a direct cost in their outcomes (lost revenue, engagement, satisfaction for that group). This is why holdouts are kept *small* (often ~1%) and why the decision to run one balances the measurement value against the cost to those users. You use a holdout when knowing the true cumulative value of your ML matters enough to justify the cost, demonstrating ML's overall business impact, catching whether your stream of "wins" actually adds up, having a stable long-term baseline, and you keep it small and reconsider it periodically. The broader lesson holdouts teach is humility about incremental gains: a parade of positive A/B tests does *not* guarantee large cumulative value, because gains overlap, fade, and sometimes do not aggregate, and the only way to know your real total impact is to hold out a baseline and measure against it. This connects to honest measurement throughout the track, just as you benchmark a model against a naive baseline, you benchmark your *entire body of ML work* against a held-out baseline, to know it genuinely created the value the individual experiments suggested. Holdouts are how mature ML organisations keep themselves honest about whether all the work actually paid off.
