===D1===
## A deployed model starts dying the day it launches
Here is the uncomfortable truth of production ML: a model deployed to production **begins degrading the moment it launches**, not because of bugs in your code, but because the world changes. The distribution of inputs shifts, the relationship between inputs and outputs changes, and over time the model's predictions drift away from accurate. A model is a snapshot of patterns at training time, and reality keeps moving after that snapshot. **Model monitoring** is the practice of detecting this degradation, and the central, dangerous fact is that without monitoring it is **invisible**: the model keeps returning confident predictions, the service shows all green, and nothing errors, while the predictions quietly get worse, until they are bad enough that users or stakeholders notice, which is the worst possible way to find out.

## Why models degrade: the world is not stationary
Unlike ordinary software, which keeps working until you change it, a model can break with *no change to your code at all*, because its correctness depends on the world matching its training data, and the world does not hold still. Two forces drive the decay, and naming them frames the week:
- **Input drift (data drift):** the distribution of the *inputs* the model sees in production diverges from what it trained on. A delay model trained on pre-pandemic flight patterns meets post-pandemic patterns; a loan model trained on 2019 economic conditions meets 2022. The inputs moved.
- **Concept drift:** the *relationship* between inputs and outputs changes, the same input now implies a different outcome, because the underlying dynamics shifted (a feature that predicted default in 2019 means something different after an economic shock).
Both silently erode accuracy, and both are about the world moving away from the training snapshot, which is why a model that was accurate at launch is not guaranteed to stay accurate, and why "we deployed it and it works" is a statement with an expiry date.

## The defining property: these are silent failures
The reason monitoring is a whole discipline, and the theme of the week, is that model degradation is a **silent failure**, you saw this danger for pipelines in W15, and it is even sharper for deployed models. A crashed server alerts you; a model whose accuracy slid from 0.85 to 0.65 does not, it returns predictions in exactly the same format, just wronger, with no error, no exception, no signal in any standard system monitor. The model looks healthy by every conventional measure (it is up, it is fast, it responds) while being increasingly *wrong*, and that gap between "looks fine" and "is fine" is precisely what model monitoring exists to close. So monitoring a model is not like monitoring a web service (is it up? is it fast?), it is about watching whether the model is still *correct*, by tracking the inputs (for drift), the predictions, and, where possible, the eventual ground truth, so that degradation raises an alarm long before users feel it. This week you build that, the early-warning system for the silent decay that every production model eventually suffers.

===D2===
## A catalogue of production failures
To monitor a model well, you need to know what you are watching *for*, so today you catalogue the ways a production model fails, beyond the obvious code bugs. The major categories:
- **Input drift (data drift):** production inputs no longer match the training distribution, the model is being asked about a world it was not trained on.
- **Concept drift:** the input-output relationship itself changed (tomorrow's topic).
- **Data quality breaks:** an upstream change feeds the model malformed inputs, a feature that is suddenly all nulls, a unit change (dollars to cents), a reordered or renamed column, so the model receives garbage and predicts garbage.
- **Outliers / out-of-distribution inputs:** individual requests unlike anything in training, where the model's prediction is unreliable but confident (D5's topic).
- **Performance/serving issues:** latency creep, errors, the operational problems from W17.
Each fails differently and needs a different signal, and most of them, crucially, produce *no error*, just wrong or unreliable predictions.

## Watching inputs when you cannot watch accuracy
The deep challenge of model monitoring, and the reason input drift gets so much attention, is that **you usually cannot measure accuracy in real time**, because the ground truth arrives late or never. When FlightWise predicts a flight will be late, you only learn if it *was* late hours later (and you may never systematically collect it); a loan default model's "correct answer" arrives months or years after the prediction. So you cannot just watch live accuracy the way you watched test AUC, the labels are not there yet. This is why monitoring leans heavily on **input drift detection**: you *can* observe the inputs immediately (you see every request), so you watch whether the input distribution is shifting away from training, as a *leading indicator* of likely accuracy loss. The logic is that if the inputs now look very different from what the model trained on, its predictions are probably less reliable, even before you can confirm it with labels. Watching the inputs is your early warning precisely because the inputs are observable now and the accuracy is not, which makes input drift the workhorse of model monitoring.

## Detecting drift: statistical distance tests
Input drift detection is fundamentally a statistical question, how different is the current input distribution from the training distribution?, and several tests measure that distance, each in its own way:
- **Population Stability Index (PSI):** a widely-used industry measure of how much a feature's distribution has shifted, with rule-of-thumb thresholds (small = stable, large = significant drift).
- **Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test:** a statistical test of whether two samples come from the same distribution, good for continuous features.
- **KL divergence / Wasserstein distance:** information-theoretic and distributional measures of how far one distribution is from another.
You compute these per feature, comparing the recent production data against the training (reference) distribution, and a feature whose drift metric crosses a threshold flags as "this input has shifted meaningfully." The practical workflow is to track these drift scores continuously and **alert** when they exceed thresholds, so a meaningful shift in the inputs raises a flag (and prompts investigation or retraining) *before* the accuracy loss becomes visible to users. You do not need to derive the statistics; you need to understand that these tests quantify "how different is now from training," that you run them per feature on a schedule, and that a crossed threshold is an early warning of likely degradation.

===D3===
## The harder failure: concept drift
Input drift is the inputs changing; **concept drift** is the *relationship* between inputs and outputs changing, the same input now implies a different outcome, and it is both subtler and harder to detect. The model learned a mapping from features to target at training time, and concept drift means that mapping is no longer correct, even if the inputs look the same. An example makes it concrete: a fraud model learns the patterns of fraud in 2022, but fraudsters change tactics in 2023, so the *same* transaction features now indicate something different, the concept of "what fraud looks like" drifted, and the model's learned relationship is stale. Crucially, this can happen with *no* input drift at all: the inputs may be distributed exactly as before, but what they *mean* for the target has shifted, so input-drift detection alone misses it.

## Why concept drift is hard to catch
Concept drift is the most insidious failure because detecting it usually *requires the ground truth*, which is exactly what you often lack in real time. Input drift you can spot by watching inputs alone (D2); but to know the input-output *relationship* changed, you generally need to compare predictions against actual outcomes, and see that the model is now wrong more often, which means you need labels. When labels arrive late or never, concept drift can progress undetected for a long time, the model is quietly making the *wrong* prediction for inputs that look perfectly normal, with no input-drift signal to warn you. This is why concept drift is the failure that most often surprises teams: nothing in the inputs looked wrong, yet the model decayed, because the world's rules changed underneath it. The forms it takes also vary, gradual drift (slow change over months), sudden drift (an abrupt shift, like a pandemic or a regulation change), or recurring/seasonal drift, and each calls for different vigilance.

## Detecting and responding to concept drift
Given that concept drift usually needs labels, the strategies for catching it center on *getting* ground truth, even partially, and watching the model's real correctness:
- **Track performance as labels arrive:** even when ground truth is delayed, it usually *does* come eventually (you learn whether the flight was late, whether the loan defaulted), so you log predictions and, when the true outcome arrives, compute the model's actual accuracy over time, a falling accuracy trend is the direct signal of concept drift (or any degradation).
- **Collect labels deliberately:** sample some predictions for human labelling, or set up feedback loops (user corrections, downstream outcomes), to get a steady, if partial, stream of ground truth for monitoring.
- **Watch proxy signals:** monitor the *prediction* distribution and downstream business metrics, if the model's output distribution shifts oddly or a business KPI tied to the model degrades, it can hint at concept drift even before labels confirm it.
The fundamental response to confirmed drift, input or concept, is **retraining** on recent data, so the model relearns the current patterns and relationships, which is why monitoring and retraining are partners: monitoring detects when the model has gone stale, and retraining refreshes it. The mature stance is to assume *every* production model will eventually drift and to build the monitoring (input drift as early warning, accuracy tracking as confirmation) and the retraining process *before* the degradation bites, rather than discovering staleness from angry users. Concept drift, the silent change in what the data means, is the deepest reason a model is never "done" once deployed.

===D4===
## What you can measure right now: online metrics
Accuracy needs labels you may not have yet, but plenty *is* observable in real time, and **online metrics** are the signals you monitor continuously on a live model. They split into what you can always see and what you can sometimes confirm:
- **Always observable (no labels needed):** the **input distributions** (for drift, D2), the **prediction distribution** (what fraction of flights is the model calling "late" this week, and is that shifting?), and **operational metrics** (latency, throughput, error rate, the W17 serving health).
- **Observable when labels arrive:** the actual **accuracy / AUC / precision-recall** over time, computed as ground truth comes in (D3), the direct correctness signal, just delayed.
A monitoring dashboard tracks all of these together, so you watch the model's inputs, outputs, performance, and (eventually) its real accuracy, the same multi-signal observability you built for AI systems in the AI-engineering track, now for a deployed ML model.

## The prediction distribution is a free early warning
A particularly useful and often-overlooked signal is the **prediction distribution**, what the model is actually outputting, because it needs no labels and reacts immediately. If FlightWise normally predicts ~18% of flights late and that suddenly jumps to 40% or drops to 5%, *something changed*, the inputs drifted, a data-quality break fed it garbage, or the world shifted, and you see it instantly in the output distribution, before any ground truth could confirm a problem. This makes the prediction distribution a cheap, real-time early-warning signal that complements input-drift detection: input drift watches what goes *in*, prediction monitoring watches what comes *out*, and a sharp change in either is a flag to investigate. It also catches failures that input monitoring might miss, a subtle data-quality break that shifts predictions even if each input feature looks individually plausible. Watching the prediction distribution is one of the highest-value, lowest-effort monitoring practices, and it works on every model regardless of label availability.

## Building the monitoring system: track, threshold, alert
The practical shape of online monitoring is the same as any good observability, **track, threshold, alert**, applied to these ML-specific signals. You continuously *track* the input distributions, prediction distribution, operational metrics, and (as labels arrive) accuracy, into a monitoring store and dashboard; you set *thresholds* for what counts as a meaningful problem (drift score above X, prediction rate shifted by more than Y, accuracy below the bar); and you *alert* when a threshold is crossed so a human investigates, rather than discovering the issue from users. The discipline that makes this work is the same as the AI-engineering drift lesson: tune thresholds so alerts mean something real (a flood of false alarms gets muted, and a muted monitor protects nothing), and watch *trends* over time, not single noisy points. The goal is an early-warning system where a drifting model, a data-quality break, or a falling accuracy *raises a flag while it is still small*, giving you time to investigate and retrain before users are affected. This turns the silent failure into a visible, alertable signal, which is the entire point of monitoring, and it is the operational backbone that keeps a deployed model trustworthy over its life, not just at launch.

===D5===
## Per-request safety: outlier detection at serving time
Distribution monitoring (D2-D4) watches the *aggregate* of inputs over time; **outlier detection at serving time** watches *each individual request* as it arrives, flagging inputs that are unlike anything the model trained on. This matters because a single out-of-distribution (OOD) input, a request whose feature values fall far outside the training data, gets a prediction from the model that is **unreliable but delivered with full confidence**, and acting on that confident-but-meaningless prediction can cause real harm. So at serving time, you check whether each incoming request is "normal" (within the realm the model can handle) or an outlier the model is not qualified to predict on, and you handle the outliers specially rather than blindly trusting the prediction.

## Why a confident prediction on a weird input is dangerous
The core danger here is that a model **always returns a prediction**, no matter how strange the input, and it does not know what it does not know. Feed FlightWise a flight with a departure hour of 47 or a route it never saw, and it will not error, it will compute *some* probability and return it with the same apparent confidence as any normal prediction, even though that prediction is essentially meaningless because the input is outside its training experience. This is the model-level version of the "garbage in, confident garbage out" problem, and it is dangerous precisely because there is no signal in the prediction itself that it should not be trusted. The W17 input validation catches *malformed* inputs (wrong type, out of range), but outlier detection catches a subtler case: inputs that are *valid* but *unusual*, each feature individually plausible, yet the combination unlike anything in training, where the model is technically able to predict but should not be relied upon. Detecting these OOD inputs at serving time, and treating their predictions with appropriate caution, is how you stop the model from confidently advising on cases it has no basis to judge.

## How to detect and handle outliers
Detecting OOD inputs reuses the unsupervised tools from W7, now at serving time per request, and handling them is a judgement about risk:
- **Detect:** score each input's "unusualness," distance from the training data's typical region (a point far from all training clusters), reconstruction error from an autoencoder trained on normal data, or a model's own uncertainty estimate. A high score flags an outlier.
- **Handle:** decide what to do with a flagged outlier rather than silently trusting it, depending on the stakes. Options include **flagging the prediction as low-confidence** (so downstream systems or users know to treat it cautiously), **routing to a human** for review (in high-stakes domains like lending or medicine, an unusual case deserves a person), **falling back** to a safe default, or at minimum **logging** the outlier for investigation (a stream of outliers may itself signal drift). 
The principle is that the model should be *honest about its limits*: when it receives an input it has no real basis to judge, the system should recognise that and respond accordingly, not launder an unreliable guess as a confident prediction. This is especially vital in high-stakes applications, where a confident wrong prediction on a weird case can cause real damage, and it complements the aggregate drift monitoring: drift detection catches the *population* shifting, outlier detection catches the *individual* request that is off, and together they keep the model from being trusted where it should not be. Knowing what the model does not know is one of the most important and underappreciated parts of trustworthy ML serving.

===D6===
## Monitoring as infrastructure: the tools
You could build model monitoring from scratch, computing drift statistics, tracking prediction distributions, storing and dashboarding metrics, but as with experiment tracking and serving, dedicated **tools** provide it as infrastructure so you do not reinvent it. The model-monitoring landscape includes purpose-built platforms (Evidently, Arize, WhyLabs, Fiddler, NannyML, and others) that specialise in exactly this: they compute drift metrics (PSI, KS, and the rest) for you, track input and prediction distributions, monitor performance as labels arrive, detect outliers, and provide dashboards and alerting tuned for ML monitoring. You instrument your serving to send it the inputs, predictions, and (when available) labels, and the platform handles the drift detection, visualisation, and alerting.

## What the dedicated tools give you
The value of a specialised model-monitoring tool over hand-rolled scripts or generic dashboards is that it understands ML monitoring's specific shape, the same "AI-native" advantage you saw with observability platforms in AI-engineering. A model-monitoring platform knows what *drift* is and computes the right statistical tests per feature out of the box; it knows to compare production against a *reference* (training) distribution; it handles the *delayed-label* reality (tracking predictions now, joining accuracy later when ground truth arrives); it detects outliers; and it renders ML-meaningful views (per-feature drift, prediction distribution over time, accuracy as labels come in) rather than generic metric graphs. Building all of this correctly, especially the statistical drift tests and the delayed-label accuracy tracking, is real work the tools have already done and battle-tested, so you instrument and configure rather than implement. This lets you get robust monitoring quickly and focus on *responding* to what it shows (investigating drift, deciding when to retrain) rather than on building the detection machinery.

## Choosing and using monitoring tools wisely
The familiar judgement applies: match the tooling to your needs, and integrate monitoring into your broader ML operations rather than treating it as a bolt-on. For a learning project or a simple model, a lightweight open-source library (like Evidently) that generates drift reports may be entirely sufficient, and a full enterprise monitoring platform would be over-engineering. For a production model serving real users with real consequences, a more complete monitoring platform with continuous tracking, alerting, and dashboards earns its place, especially in regulated or high-stakes domains where undetected drift is costly. The hosted-vs-self-hosted choice recurs here too (send your production data to a vendor, or self-host for privacy), the same trade you have navigated throughout. The most important point is that monitoring must **connect to action**: a drift alert is only valuable if it triggers a response, investigation, and retraining, so you wire monitoring into your operations (alerts reach the right people, drift triggers a retraining workflow) rather than letting it produce dashboards nobody acts on. Monitoring tooling provides the detection; your job is to ensure the detected problems lead to fixes, closing the loop from "the model is drifting" to "the model has been retrained," which is what actually keeps a production model accurate over its life.
